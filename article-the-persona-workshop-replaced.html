CONSUMER INSIGHTS

The persona workshop, replaced

Personas built in a room describe the people in the room. Built from observed affinities, they describe an audience — and they can be checked.

15 July 20262 min readLicter analysis team

Cet article n’est disponible qu’en anglais. Le reste du site bascule en français.

What the workshop actually produces

The classic persona exercise collects a team’s assumptions, formats them, and gives them a name and a stock photograph. The output feels like knowledge because it is written down, and it is very hard to argue with because there is nothing in it that can be wrong.

That is the real cost. Not that the personas are inaccurate, but that they are unfalsifiable, so they survive contact with every subsequent piece of evidence.

The problem is not that workshop personas are inaccurate. It is that they are unfalsifiable.

What observed segmentation changes

Building a segmentation from affinities, interests and overlaps means every segment has a size, a boundary and a set of things that are true of it. All three can be wrong, which means all three can be corrected.

It also produces segments nobody in the room would have proposed. The overlap between two audiences you assumed were distinct is a common one, and it is usually where the reach upside sits.

Keep the workshop, change its input

This is not an argument for deleting the workshop. The room is where the segmentation gets connected to product decisions, and that connection has to be made by people who own those decisions.

The change is what the room starts from. Walk in with observed segments and let the team argue about what to do with them, instead of walking in empty and letting the team invent the audience.

What to take away

  • Workshop personas encode the room’s assumptions and cannot be disproved.
  • Observed segments have a size, a boundary and checkable properties.
  • Unexpected overlaps are usually where reach upside sits.
  • Keep the workshop; change what it starts from.
