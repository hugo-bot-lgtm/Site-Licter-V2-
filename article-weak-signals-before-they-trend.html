Weak signals, before they trend

Most of what gets called a weak signal is just a small number. Here is the difference between a conversation that predicts a shift and one that only looks like it does.

The problem with small numbers

Every monitoring tool can show you a topic going from forty mentions to four hundred. That is a multiplication by ten, and it will be flagged as a spike by any threshold you set. It is also, almost always, nothing: a single account with reach, one article syndicated across a group, a hashtag borrowed by an unrelated community for a week.

A weak signal is not a small volume. It is a small volume that behaves in a particular way, and the behaviour is what you have to look for. Volume alone will hand you a list of accidents and call it foresight.

A weak signal is not a small volume. It is a small volume behaving in a particular way.

Three properties worth checking

It spreads across communities rather than inside one. A topic that triples inside a single community is a community being loud. A topic that appears in three communities that do not normally talk to each other, at low volume in each, is a topic finding structural purchase. The second one is much rarer and much more interesting.

It survives its first news cycle. Anything can peak. What matters is the floor afterwards. If the baseline after the peak is meaningfully above the baseline before it, something was retained. If it returns to exactly where it was, the conversation borrowed the topic and gave it back.

It changes vocabulary as it travels. A signal that matters gets reworded by each community that picks it up, because each one has to fit it to its own concerns. A signal that travels with identical phrasing everywhere is usually being distributed rather than adopted.

What this costs you in practice

Checking those three properties is not free. It means keeping a perimeter stable for long enough to have a real baseline, which rules out rebuilding your query every quarter. It means segmenting the conversation into communities before you measure it, not after. And it means accepting that most of what you look at will turn out to be noise, which is uncomfortable when someone is waiting for a finding.

The alternative is worse. A team that reports every spike burns its credibility inside two quarters, and by the time it sees something real nobody is listening.

What to do with one when you find it

Do not escalate it as a trend. Escalate it as a hypothesis with a date attached: here is what we think is forming, here is what we expect to see in six weeks if we are right, here is what would disprove it. Then go back and check.

That last step is the one that almost never happens, and it is the only one that makes the practice improve. A foresight function that never scores its own calls is producing opinions with charts.

What to take away

  • Spread across unconnected communities beats growth inside one.
  • Compare the floor after the peak to the floor before it.
  • Vocabulary that mutates as it travels is a sign of adoption, not distribution.
  • Escalate hypotheses with a review date, not trends.
